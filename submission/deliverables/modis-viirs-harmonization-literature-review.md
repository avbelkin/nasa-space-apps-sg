# Harmonizing MODIS and VIIRS Data: A Review of Existing Approaches and a Hypothesis on the Role of Detailed Land-Surface Type

**PDF version:** [on GitHub](Literature-Review_MODIS-VIIRS-Harmonization.pdf) · [on Google Drive](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1iEgg_tIgG9B2vq1-lW3SPwoRwGsT2GRf/view)

**Scope:** 82 cited sources and a proposed research hypothesis. Implementation status checked against Earth Fire Calendar commit `7bbe91a` on 15 November 2026; current functionality and evaluation are documented in the [technical note](technical-note.md). A proposal below is not a claim that the app implements or validates that method.

## 1. Introduction: three fundamental barriers to MODIS–VIIRS comparability

The NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026 problem statement [S318] spells out why the more than twenty-year MODIS archive (Terra since 2000, Aqua since 2002) and the records of the new-generation VIIRS (Suomi NPP since 2012, NOAA-20 since 2018, NOAA-21 since 2024; see [S294]; [S260]) cannot be combined by simply merging tables of active-fire hotspots: according to the statement, the two records cannot be compared directly without harmonization, because MODIS and VIIRS differ in spatial resolution, sensitivity and observation schedules [S318].

The raw counts of the two sensors differ severalfold: across South America in 2012–2021, S-NPP VIIRS 375 m recorded $5.13$ times more fire pixels than Aqua MODIS [S268].

A review of 77 sources (papers, user guides, technical notes and software repositories), plus five more sources from the radar remote-sensing literature that appear in §5.2 only as examples of land-cover maps used in other tasks, shows that there is a lot of work comparing MODIS and VIIRS, but that harmonization methods proper have a single central line — **geometric and statistical conversion between the two pixel systems**: projecting detections into a pixel footprint, view-zenith-angle (VZA) corrections, quantile mapping, observation operators, and aggregation onto a common grid (§2). Other groups of studies deal with overpass schedules, the diurnal cycle and orbit drift (§3), and with spectral and atmospheric attenuation of the signal (§4); among the latter, the work of Oliva & Schroeder [S258] on absorption in the $\text{CO}_2$ band stands out. Land-surface type is also present in the literature (§5): in validation, in the stratification of comparisons by land-cover maps, in the contextual tests of the algorithms themselves and — jointly with the position of a detection in the scan — in the GFED5NRT corrections [S198]. The niche of our project (§6) is therefore stated narrowly: not "nobody has accounted for land cover", but **testing whether the angular dependence of the MODIS–VIIRS inter-sensor response differs between detailed surface classes at the level of individual detections, and whether accounting for this improves the result relative to the corrections that already exist**.

## 2. Spatial resolution, scan geometry and geometric-statistical conversion methods

### 2.1. Pixel growth toward the swath edge, scan overlaps and the point spread function
The first difference stems from the opto-mechanical scanning design:
* **MODIS** has a nominal resolution of $\sim 1\text{ km}$ at nadir, but toward the swath edge the pixel area grows from $1$ to $9.6\text{ km}^2$ because of panoramic elongation [S207]. In addition, because a fire shifts within the point spread function (PSF), the standard deviation of the difference in fire radiative power (FRP) for the same fire in adjacent overlapping Terra/Aqua scans is $26.6\%$ ($1\sigma$) at the level of a single 1-km pixel and decreases only with spatial or temporal aggregation [S234]. Inter-scan duplicates (the *bow-tie* effect) contribute up to $18.3\%$ of cumulative FRP for Aqua MODIS versus $6.7\%$ for VIIRS 750 m [S199].
* **VIIRS 375 m** (bands I1–I5) uses onboard three-zone detector aggregation: the effective footprint is about $383 \times 360\text{ m}$ at nadir and grows only to $\sim 795 \times 784\text{ m}$ at the swath edge, while onboard duplicate removal (*bow-tie deletion*) replaces 4 rows in the second zone and 8 rows in the third zone with fill values [S221]. Already in the first comparisons of the 750-m S-NPP VIIRS product with Aqua MODIS, the VIIRS aggregation scheme yielded $26\%$ more detections in the central zone and $70\%$ more at the outer scan angles [S244].
* Experiments with the MODIS Airborne Simulator (MAS, $50\text{ m}$ resolution) over the Brazilian cerrado [S265] showed quantitatively that degrading the pixel to $1\text{ km}$ causes most small clusters to be missed, but because weak fires carry less energy, 1-km pixels retain $72\%$ of total fire radiative energy (FRE) over the five-hour campaign and $65\text{–}71\%$ of the reference FRP during the daytime Aqua overpass (whereas 200-m pixels retain $>97\%$ of FRP).

A number of conversion methods have been developed in the literature to compensate for these geometric differences:

### 2.2. Projection into the MODIS pixel footprint and polynomial VZA correction
Zheng et al. (2026) [S207] propose a *correction-before-fusion* framework for daytime/nighttime Aqua `MYD14` and S-NPP `VNP14IMG` observations for 2012–2024. The authors remove scan duplicates, aggregate near-simultaneous VIIRS points directly into the native MODIS pixel footprint ($1\text{–}9.6\text{ km}^2$), introduce a statistical cloud correction of VIIRS FRP, and fit the mean FRP ratio (VIIRS / MODIS) over $1^\circ$ bins of MODIS view zenith angle (VZA) with a second-degree polynomial. After the multiplicative correction of MODIS, a quality-priority fusion is performed (corrected VIIRS is chosen where available, otherwise corrected MODIS).

### 2.3. Comparisons across the swath, by cluster and on a hierarchy of regular grids
* **Li et al. (2018)** [S199] compared Aqua MODIS (`MYD14`) and the **750-m** S-NPP VIIRS product (`NDEAF-L2`) for 2016–2017 at four scales (without projection into the MODIS pixel footprint): by $1^\circ$ VZA bins for overpasses coinciding within $\pm 5\text{ min}$, by 619 manually delineated fire clusters (time difference $<20\text{ min}$, VZA difference $<15^\circ$), by continent and on a global $1^\circ$ grid. As VZA increased from nadir to $60^\circ$, the number of daytime MODIS detections fell by $67\%$, while mean FRP per pixel rose from $22$ to $90\text{ MW}$ ($+300\%$) because small fires were screened out. An important constraint for what follows: in the matched clusters themselves, the MODIS / VIIRS FRP ratio showed no dependence on VZA over the $0\text{–}60^\circ$ range in any of the five ecosystems examined.
* **Li et al. (2020)** [S202] compared daytime Aqua MODIS with the **375-m** S-NPP VIIRS product (`VNP14IMG`) over Africa on seven regular grids ($0.05^\circ, 0.1^\circ, 0.25^\circ, 0.5^\circ, 1^\circ, 2.5^\circ, 5^\circ$) for 44 days with an overpass difference of $\le 5\text{ min}$. The minimum per-pixel FRP of MODIS grew from $4.3\text{ MW}$ at nadir to $>31.7\text{ MW}$ at the swath edge (versus $1.3 \to 2.9\text{ MW}$ for VIIRS). The authors built a quadratic regression of the median gridded FRP ratio on VZA (in radians) for cells where both sensors recorded fire, and separately estimated the MODIS losses in the equatorial inter-orbit *swath gaps*, which accounted for $10.7\%$ of annual VIIRS FRP (overall, daytime MODIS FRP in Africa was at least $42.8\%$ lower than VIIRS).
* Conceptually similar scan-geometry corrections for MODIS itself have been proposed through *nadir-equivalent looks* [S284] and through an empirical factor of $1.44$ compensating for the *bow-tie* effect in monthly CMG grids ([S217]; [S286]). The factor was derived for MODIS Collection 5 and is justified for monthly sums at the level of countries and larger regions of Africa, not as a universal multiplier for any cell.

### 2.4. Within-sensor quantile mapping
Kaur et al. (2019) [S229] apply empirical inverse quantile mapping (CDF matching) to correct the swath-edge underestimation of FRP in MODIS products (on the $0.1^\circ$ GFAS grid). It is important to stress that **the calibration here is done within MODIS itself**: FRP distributions in 10 angular zones ($0^\circ\text{–}65^\circ$) are mapped to the near-nadir MODIS distribution ($\text{VZA} < 12^\circ$) using 2016 data, while co-located 750-m VIIRS for 2017 is used only for independent evaluation of the result. Since the method cannot recover "false zeros" (cells where MODIS missed the fire entirely), the authors derive the factors on a coarser $1^\circ$ grid and interpolate them back to $0.1^\circ$, which transfers the energy of missed fires to neighbouring cells at distances of up to $\sim 100\text{ km}$. **Land-cover type does not enter the correction function**: it depends only on FRP magnitude and view angle, while eight land-cover classes (from GFEDv3.1) are used only in the evaluation, which showed that the quality of the correction at regional level depends on land-cover type.

### 2.5. Observation operators based on pixel area and cloudiness
Sofiev & Kouznetsov (2026) [S200] and the `FRP_OOp` software archives ([S314]; [S327]) build pixel-level and gridded observation operators for MODIS Collection 6.1 and VIIRS Collections 2/2.1. Detection thresholds are determined from the inflection point of fitted logistic curves (sigmoids) of the FRP distribution and are expressed as a linear function of pixel area:
* For the smallest (near-nadir) pixels, the daytime and nighttime detection thresholds are estimated at $5.7 / 3.7\text{ MW}$ for MODIS and $3.7 / 0.7\text{ MW}$ for VIIRS (the paper's abstract separately gives $0.5\text{ MW}$ as the smallest reliably detectable nighttime VIIRS fire).
* For MODIS the detection threshold varies from $3.7\text{ MW}$ (clear night at nadir) to $40\text{ MW}$ (daytime at the swath edge).
* The gridded operator, in addition to pixel area, accounts for the share of the largest fire pixel in a cell and the fraction of omissions due to cloud and sun glint from the `MxD35` masks (by the authors' estimate, the global mean detection probability for strong fires is $78\%$ for MODIS and $88\%$ for VIIRS, and in some equatorial areas less than $30\%$ and $40\%$). With symmetric cross-mapping of the operators over >40,000 granules for 2020, the raw discrepancy in total signal ($128\text{ TW}$ for MODIS versus $177\text{ TW}$ for VIIRS) shrinks to $88$ and $106\text{ TW}$, and the authors attribute the remainder to the difference in overpass time.

## 3. The third axis of harmonization: observation schedules, the diurnal cycle and orbit drift

As the challenge statement [S318] rightly points out, the difference in *observation schedules* is no less critical than pixel size:

1. **The diurnal cycle of burning and the sparsity of polar-orbiting sampling:**
   An analysis of 86 months of TRMM VIRS data supplemented by Terra MODIS observations [S205] showed that in the tropics and subtropics the diurnal peak of burning falls at 13:00–18:30 local time, so the morning Terra overpass ($\sim 10{:}30$) and the afternoon Aqua and VIIRS overpasses ($\sim 13{:}30$) sample different phases of the diurnal cycle; the size of this difference depends on the region [S208]. The cost of sparse sampling can be estimated from geostationary observations by SEVIRI at 15-min intervals ([S213]; [S217]; [S286]) and Himawari-8 AHI at 10-min intervals [S243]. In the experiment of Andela et al. [S213], the SEVIRI record over Africa and the Mediterranean for 2010–2012 was thinned to the moments when a MODIS observation was available and compared with the full SEVIRI record (VIIRS was not part of the experiment): piecewise-constant carry-over of the last measured FRP between overpasses gave $163\%$ of the reference fire radiative energy (FRE), i.e. an overestimate of $63\%$, whereas a climatological Gaussian parameterization of the diurnal cycle gave $97\%$, i.e. it substantially reduced the aggregate bias (to $-3\%$) but did not eliminate it. Moreover, the ratio of daytime to nighttime burning itself changes over time: in the United States in 2003–2020 the share of nighttime FRP grew mainly because of large class G fires ($\ge 2023\text{ ha}$), which produce $89\%$ of all nighttime FRP [S246]; part of the observed growth may reflect changes in detectability rather than only in the fire regime itself.
2. **Orbit drift and changes in the satellite constellation:**
   A modelled example for the NOAA AVHRR satellites shows that gradual orbit drift shifts the overpass time relative to the diurnal peak of burning and can produce apparent, almost tenfold changes with an unchanged daily number of fires [S205]. In the observed NOAA-14 record over Europe, the number of detected fires fell by roughly $90\%$ over the satellite's lifetime, although other factors also affect that record [S206]. Orbit-drift artifacts have also been found in the AVHRR-based FireCCILT11 burned-area product [S218].
   A similar process is now under way for MODIS itself, and the different kinds of orbit maintenance were discontinued at different times. Inclination adjust maneuvers (IAM), which held the local overpass time, were last performed on 27 February 2020 for Terra and 18 March 2021 for Aqua; drag make-up maneuvers (DMU), which held the altitude and ground-track repeat, on 28 July 2022 for Terra and 1 December 2021 for Aqua; in October 2022 Terra exited the constellation to a lower orbit ([S197], Table 1; [S270]). As a result both satellites are drifting: ground-track repeatability is lost, the solar geometry changes and the local observation time shifts ([S197]; [S270]; [S275]; [S276]; [S312]). Studies [S197] and [S270] address the effect of the drift on the calibration of the reflective bands; the radiometric calibration of the instruments and its consistency between MODIS and VIIRS [S271] is a separate problem that should not be conflated with the harmonization of fire products. Because of the ageing and drift of MODIS, the global CAMS GFAS system began ingesting VIIRS data on 4 December 2025 and is being adapted to operational Sentinel-3 FRP data ([S277]; for a nighttime inter-sensor comparison of SLSTR with MODIS and VIIRS see [S210]). A generational change has also taken place within the VIIRS constellation itself: on 2 November 2026 data delivery from Suomi NPP was permanently discontinued, with users transitioned to NOAA-21 and NOAA-20 [S311].
3. **Precedents for stitching and merging records from different sensors:**
   The problem of joining records arose before VIIRS as well: in GFED4, ATSR and VIRS active-fire counts are converted to burned area by regressions built on MODIS-era data [S214], and the description of the GFED5 dataset explicitly warns of three breaks in the record at the sensor transitions — in 1998, 2001 and 2003 [S325]. For emission estimates, FRP from polar-orbiting sensors is merged with geostationary data: ABI is calibrated against VIIRS and combined with it on a 3-km grid in the RAVE product [S253], and VIIRS is combined with AHI for Indonesia [S254] and Australia [S252]. In fire-growth mapping, MODIS and VIIRS detections are often simply pooled without inter-sensor corrections ([S256]; [S262]).

## 4. Spectral and atmospheric attenuation of the mid-infrared signal

Beyond pixel geometry and overpass schedules, a separate group of studies shows that part of the FRP differences between MODIS and VIIRS is related to the **spectral characteristics of their IR bands and to atmospheric absorption**:

1. **Overlap with the $\text{CO}_2$ absorption band (Oliva & Schroeder, 2015 [S258]; Li et al., 2018 [S199]; NASA VIIRS Land [S308]):**
   In the MODIS algorithms FRP is computed from bands 21/22 (nominally $3.929\text{–}3.989\text{ µm}$), and in the standard Level-2 product atmospheric transmittance is taken to be $1$, although absorption grows with scan angle ([S220], pp. 6–7). For VIIRS, the FRP computation relies on band M13 ($3.973\text{–}4.128\text{ µm}$; ranges from [S272], Table 1), whose spectral response overlaps more strongly with the carbon dioxide absorption band ($\text{CO}_2$, $4\text{–}5\text{ µm}$), which causes stronger attenuation of the signal ([S258]; [S308]).
   Oliva & Schroeder [S258] performed an atmospheric correction in MODTRAN4, using NCEP GFS meteorological fields, for paired Aqua MODIS and S-NPP VIIRS 750 m detections (selected within $\le 1\text{ km}$ of each other at similar overpass times and similar VZA). In 4 of 6 regions the ratio of total FRP (MODIS / VIIRS) moved closer to unity (over the whole sample of 3949 VIIRS detections and 3542 MODIS detections the ratio changed from $1.22$ to $1.01$), but in two regions with few small fires (Angola and the central United States) agreement remained poor. Li et al. [S199] also invoke absorption in $\text{CO}_2$-rich smoke to explain why, in intense boreal clusters ($>1000\text{ MW}$), MODIS shows $47\%$ higher FRP than VIIRS.
2. **Angular dependence of gaseous absorption, water vapour and the residual daytime bias (Zhou et al., 2023 [S273]; Deng et al., 2024 [S238]; `uiowa-aer-lab` code [S316]):**
   The FILDA-2 algorithm for nighttime VIIRS data (Zhou et al., [S273]) jointly uses the day/night band (DNB), the I- and M-bands, dynamic contextual thresholds and per-pixel FRP estimation; according to the authors, it reduces the angular dependence of FRP estimates and the *bow-tie* effect compared with the standard I-band product. Deng et al. [S238] show that the single-channel $4\text{ µm}$ method underestimates *smoldering* fires (to correct for this, band M14 at $8.55\text{ µm}$ was added in FILDA-2), and build atmospheric transmittance lookup tables (UNL-VRTM) for MODIS AF, VIIRS AF and FILDA-2 as a function of VZA, precipitable water (TQV from GEOS-FP) and surface pressure (a software implementation for VIIRS FILDA-2 is provided in the repository [S316]).
   The key result of [S238] is expressed through the ratio of mean FRP density at nadir ($\text{VZA} < 10^\circ$) to the density at the swath edge ($\text{VZA} > 60^\circ$) — this is not a MODIS / VIIRS ratio. **At night**, the atmospheric correction almost eliminates the angular skew, reducing this ratio from $1.24$ to $1.07$ for MODIS AF, from $1.55$ to $1.05$ for VIIRS AF and from $1.51$ to $1.03$ for FILDA-2. **In the daytime the angular dependence persists even after correction** ($1.45 \to 1.25$ for MODIS and $1.77 \to 1.21$ for VIIRS). The authors tentatively attribute the daytime residual to the MODIS AF and VIIRS AF daytime detection algorithms themselves: the contextual thresholds depend strongly on angle, and background pixels may contain fire signal, especially at nadir. Differences between surface types were not assessed in that work, and the correction considered does not exhaust the influence of smoke, aerosol and terrain.

## 5. Direct sensor comparisons, the role of the land surface and static heat sources in the existing literature

Contrary to the simplified notion that the land surface is ignored in the literature, the review shows that **land cover and background properties are already actively used in four capacities**:

### 5.1. Evidence that omission depends on fire size and land-cover type
Classic studies validating MODIS against ASTER ($30\text{ m}$) and Landsat ($30\text{ m}$) imagery and agency statistics showed that the probability of detection depends above all on fire size and the spatial clustering of fires, as well as on region, vegetation and observing conditions:
* In a Siberian sample, a 50% probability of MODIS detection required about 60 thirty-metre ASTER pixels in a single cluster within a MODIS pixel; the authors attribute the differences from the Amazonian results primarily to dense smoke [S245]. In the global validation of MODIS C6 [S220] the overall omission error was $86.2\%$, falling below $10\%$ only with $\ge 140$ ASTER pixels, and the probability of detection varied from $7\%$ in the Middle East to $26\%$ in Australia.
* In the United States, for 361 fires with Landsat perimeters, 50% event detectability was reached at a final fire size of about $105\text{ ha}$ for Terra+Aqua combined ($195\text{ ha}$ for Aqua alone and $334\text{ ha}$ for Terra alone; [S248]). A comparison with roughly 250,000 agency reports showed that MODIS products detect from $3.5\%$ (burned area, eastern US) to $23.4\%$ (active fires, western US) of documented fires larger than $1\text{ ha}$, and that omissions depend on leaf area index (LAI), cloudiness and region [S204].
* A comparison of MODIS hotspots with Landsat perimeters in 9 regions of the world showed that the number of hectares of burned area per hotspot differs strongly between regions; the authors relate this to the shares of `GLC2000` land-cover classes, but the study does not provide separate coefficients for each class [S203].
* A joint analysis of MODIS and VIIRS showed that the accuracy of dating burns from active fires is high in boreal and Mediterranean biomes but drops in grasslands and savannas [S257].
* In regional comparisons VIIRS 375 m usually yields more detections than MODIS, especially for small and low-intensity fires: in agricultural Punjab [S232], in the Brazilian cerrado [S222], in protected parks of South Africa [S274], in Alaska ([S267] — on average; the authors note events where, owing to the overpass schedule, MODIS yields more detections) and in the countries of South and Southeast Asia ([S266] — averages over different periods: 2003–2016 for MODIS and 2012–2016 for VIIRS). In northern India a comparison with household surveys showed that VIIRS captures crop-residue burning more completely than MODIS, although overpass time also plays a substantial role [S216]. This advantage is not universal and is measured by different quantities (number of pixels, event detectability, F1): in cloudy and mountainous Sichuan the overall F1 score is higher for VIIRS, but for fires smaller than $50\text{ ha}$ MODIS and VIIRS are practically equal ($0.162$ and $0.160$; [S249]), and in Eastern China it was not the standard product but a dedicated regional algorithm combining the I- and M-bands that yielded 5–10 times more detections of stubble burning [S272].
* Non-vegetation sources have been compared using other VIIRS products, and these results cannot be attributed to the 375-m resolution: volcanoes — with the early 750-m product [S241] and with bands M13/M15 in the MIROVA system [S223]; gas flares in Khanty-Mansiysk — with MODIS, the VIIRS 750-m product and VIIRS Nightfire, whose advantage the authors attribute to the shortwave IR band [S264].

### 5.2. Stratification of inter-sensor comparisons by land-cover maps (including ESA / Copernicus products)
A number of studies directly split the MODIS–VIIRS comparison by surface class:
* In Northeast Asia, **Fu et al. (2020) [S201]** used the annual **300-m ESA CCI / Copernicus Land Cover maps** to separate forests from low-biomass lands (cropland, meadows, herbaceous vegetation). They found that in low-biomass lands $82.8\%$ of daytime VIIRS detections have no Aqua MODIS match (versus $65.2\%$ in forests); for simultaneous (paired) fires VIIRS FRP is lower than MODIS FRP (regression slope $0.70\text{–}0.73$), but total monthly FRP over all detections is higher for VIIRS (slope $1.22$ in forests and $1.32$ in low-biomass lands).
* In Turkey, **Coskuner (2022) [S226]** applied the **100-m ESA Copernicus Global Land Service (CGLS) maps** with five land-cover classes (closed forest, open forest, shrubland, herbaceous vegetation, cropland) and three fire-size classes, showing a sharp drop in the detectability of fires $<10\text{ ha}$ and limitations under *closed canopy*.
* Outside the fire domain, the Copernicus Global Land Cover maps (100 m) are used in radar applications mostly as a mask or filter: to exclude unsuitable classes when mapping flooded forests [S005], to select soil-moisture stations by land-cover type [S012], as the base land-cover layer in the OPERA DSWx-S1 surface-water product (according to the description of its ancillary data; [S031]) and to exclude dry tropical forests from the forest mask of the Congo Basin [S062]; in global Sentinel-1 flood monitoring the same map is used for stratification — to analyse likelihoods and the consistency of results by land-cover class [S032].
* Land cover is also attached to individual events or detections: 30-m LANDFIRE classes to fires in [S204], ESA CCI classes to active-fire records in [S266], GlobeLand30 and OpenStreetMap maps to filter candidates in [S272]; in [S243] matched observations are compared by land cover on the AHI pixel grid.
* The `MCD12Q1` land-cover maps and `GFED` biomes are used to stratify cluster-level FRP regressions [S199] and to adapt the `VNP64A1` burned-area algorithm ([S215]; with specific caveats about discrepancies over cropland in [S260] and about bias due to small burns in [S297]).
* The original `GFED5` [S219] is built on MODIS products: `MCD64A1` area is corrected against high-resolution reference data and supplemented with an estimate of missed area from `MCD14ML` active fires outside burn scars; the coefficients are stratified by region, 17 vegetation classes and tree cover, and there are separate components for cropland, peatland and deforestation. **`GFED5NRT`** ([S198]; code [S315]) extends it with VIIRS data and is a direct example of geometry and land cover being accounted for jointly: detections are attached to a 500-m land-cover map (16 classes), and the weighting factors are defined simultaneously by cross-scan sample number (0–6399), day/night, month and the dominant land cover of the $0.25^\circ$ cell, and are applied to individual VIIRS detections. The limitations of this approach for our problem: its operational input is VIIRS only (the factors were derived from the overlap with the MODIS-based `GFED5`), it uses the dominant class of a large cell, and the target quantity is burned area; the method does not directly estimate the MODIS / VIIRS FRP ratio.
* For geostationary GOES over the Brazilian Amazon, a per-pixel probabilistic cloud correction was proposed that accounts for previous fire activity, precipitation and land use [S233]: relative to burn-scar maps in the state of Acre, the omission error fell from $58.3\%$ to $43.7\%$.

### 5.3. Built-in contextual surface filters, band saturation and sun glint
The MODIS Collection 6/6.1 ([S220]; [S282]) and VIIRS 375 m Collection 2 ([S221]; [S278]) algorithms themselves embed different rules for handling difficult backgrounds, which behave differently over the same surface types:
* **Band saturation under intense burning:** for VIIRS 375 m, the primary detection band I4 ($3.55\text{–}3.93\text{ µm}$) has a low saturation threshold ($367\text{ K}$), which in a global sample was reached in $\sim 8\%$ of daytime and $1\%$ of nighttime fire pixels (and for very intense fires the digital count *folds* down to as low as $208\text{ K}$; see [S221], pp. 4, 9). This is why FRP in the VIIRS 375-m product is computed not from band I4 but from the co-located 750-m dual-gain band M13, followed by apportioning among the sub-pixels; if band M13 also saturates, or there are not enough valid background pixels in the window, the detected fire is assigned $\text{FRP} = 0$ ([S278], p. 8).
* **Reflective surfaces, forest clearings, deserts and *sun glint*:** MODIS C6 introduced a dedicated daytime *forest clearings* test (thresholds on $T_{11}$, $0.86\text{ µm}$ reflectance $> 0.28$ and $T_4 < 325\text{ K}$); overall, the transition from C5 to C6 reduced the global daytime commission error from $2.4\%$ to $1.2\%$ ([S220], pp. 5, 8). The VIIRS 375-m algorithm ([S221], pp. 5–7) applies separate tests to screen out bright non-fire objects (large reflective building roofs, concrete — these accounted for up to $6\%$ of daytime low-confidence pixels, and in Eastern China false alarms among them reached almost $40\%$), a test for mixed desert backgrounds, and rejection or confidence downgrading at small sun-glint angles $\theta_g < 15^\circ\text{–}25^\circ$. In addition, false detections can be caused by specular reflection from solar panels ([S294], p. 4) and bare soil ([S232], p. 12).

### 5.4. Static (industrial and anthropogenic) heat sources
A separate class of studies deals with persistent thermal anomalies — steel plants, quarries, oil refining and gas flares ([S235]; [S236]; [S264]; [S287]; [S288]; [S294]). In a catalogue built from nighttime VIIRS Nightfire data, $15{,}199$ industrial sites produced $49.52\%$ of the higher-confidence nighttime anomalies (a share by number of detections, not by FRP; [S235]). By the estimate of Roberts ([S288]; the Copernicus cropland fraction was among the ancillary data used to identify static anomalies), static anomalies account for $2\text{–}3\%$ of global and $20\text{–}30\%$ of nighttime FRP for VIIRS, and for $0.3\text{–}2\%$ of daytime and $6\text{–}18\%$ of nighttime FRP for MODIS; only the nighttime estimates are directly comparable, and by them the contribution of static sources is markedly higher for the more sensitive VIIRS. External catalogues [S236] and the experimental FIRMS static thermal anomaly mask, which includes more than just anthropogenic sources [S287], are used to separate such sources from wildland fires.

## 6. Synthesis of the literature and statement of our project's hypothesis

Comparing the lines of work reviewed above lets us separate what has already been done from what remains open:

1. **The central line of methods is geometric and statistical conversion** between pixel systems: observation operators [S200], the polynomial *correction-before-fusion* adjustment [S207], the quadratic angular model of the gridded FRP ratio [S202] and quantile mapping [S229]. In these correction functions the angular dependence is built without separation by land-surface type; in [S229] land cover is used only to evaluate the result. Study [S199] is a comparative study that does not propose an inter-sensor angular correction (see item 3).
2. **Land-cover type is accounted for, including jointly with geometry.** Comparisons are stratified by land-cover maps ([S201]; [S226]; [S199]), land cover is attached to individual events and detections ([S204]; [S266]), and in `GFED5NRT` [S198] the corrections are defined simultaneously by scan position and the dominant land cover of the cell. Joint treatment of land cover and geometry is therefore not novel in itself.
3. **There is a result that constrains the hypothesis:** in the matched clusters of Li et al. [S199] the MODIS / VIIRS FRP ratio did not depend on VZA in any of the five ecosystems. It was obtained for matched clusters and the 750-m product and does not rule out an effect for individual detections, missed fires and the 375-m product, but it must be taken into account.
4. According to Deng et al. [S238], after correction for gaseous absorption **the daytime "nadir / swath edge" FRP density ratio remains appreciable** ($1.25$ for MODIS and $1.21$ for VIIRS); the authors tentatively attribute this to the angular dependence of the contextual thresholds and to a background that is not perfectly "clean". The link between this residual and surface type is our conjecture: it was not tested in that work.

### The testable hypothesis of our project

We hypothesize that **the residual discrepancy between the detections and FRP of the MODIS and VIIRS sensors (especially in the daytime and at oblique view angles) depends on the interaction "detailed land-surface class $\times$ observation geometry" at the level of individual detections**. Land cover, geometry and observing conditions are already accounted for in existing approaches, including jointly in `GFED5NRT`; what remains to be tested is whether a more detailed surface classification improves the estimate of the MODIS–VIIRS inter-sensor response compared with those corrections, and whether the effect persists under comparable observing conditions.

In contrast to global angular curves and corrections based on the dominant class of a $0.25^\circ$ cell, we propose to:
* Attach each detection (with its actual `scan`/`track` dimensions, VZA, time of day and static-source flag) to a detailed land-cover class from the global **ESA CCI Land Cover ($300\text{ m}$) / Copernicus CGLS ($100\text{ m}$)** maps (by analogy with how these maps were used for regional error assessment in [S201] and [S226]).
* Test on overlapping overpasses whether the shape of the angular dependence of detection probability and of the FRP ratio (MODIS / VIIRS) differs statistically significantly between surface classes — for example, between closed forests, shrublands and open herbaceous woodlands (where daytime heating of bare soil is presumably stronger), agricultural land, deserts and mosaic transition zones (clearings, built-up areas, shores of water bodies with sun glint).
* If an improvement is confirmed on independent validation, use the resulting class-angular response functions (together with normalization for the overpass schedule and filtering of static thermal anomalies) for a harmonized burning calendar that accounts for land-cover heterogeneity; the quality of dating and of event duration should be assessed separately from sensor agreement and total FRP.

The limitations of such a test follow from the review itself: detection probability, number of fire pixels, FRP and burned area are different quantities; a result for matched detections does not describe missed events, whose assessment requires information on observations that took place without a detection, or independent reference data; the class at the pixel centre is not the composition of a large oblique footprint, and a 100–300 m map does not by itself remove mixing within the thermal pixel; individual 375-m detections are not independent FRP measurements, because several I-pixels share a single M13 measurement (§5.3), and this must be taken into account when assessing significance; a "day / night" split does not replace the continuous illumination geometry.

### What the current prototype implements

The live app reads the latest 24 hours from MODIS on Terra/Aqua and VIIRS on NOAA-20/NOAA-21. It estimates geometry from orbital elements, samples the fixed 2019 ESA CCI / C3S land-cover map (300 m native grid; 1.2 km overview for the worldwide feed), and retrieves available weather at acquisition. The pinned surface bar shows class fractions and classified coverage; for a group it averages available detection summaries. The sidebar compares raw detection counts by dominant surface class, and a CSV download exports the loaded sensor-filtered detections with their available surface fractions. Neither display nor export applies an inter-sensor correction.

The app's one-day pass-matching and FRP baselines, including the GWIS and CAMS variants, are a first harmonization step built on these fields: experimental cross-satellite corrections. They do not yet test the class-by-angle hypothesis; the archive, the class-specific angular response functions and a validated unified record come next. The model results and their limits are in [technical note §6](technical-note.md#6-first-baselines-on-matched-passes).
